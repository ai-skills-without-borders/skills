Workshops

Writing AI skills

Participants turn a teaching task they know well into a skill, test it, have a colleague try it, and share it in the library.

Present the slides Download PowerPoint Printable handouts

At a glance

Level
Advanced
Length
2 hours
For
Educators who have used at least one AI skill, in the first workshop or on their own. No programming needed; writing a skill means writing clear instructions in plain English.
Group
6 to 24 participants, working alone or in pairs. One helper for every 8 participants.
Who can lead it
Anyone who has written at least one skill and submitted it to the library. The plan, the script, the prompts and the slides are all here.
Participants leave with
Every participant leaves with a tested draft skill for a task from their own work, a peer test of it, and the skill submitted to the shared library with their institution credited.

Learning objectives

By the end, participants will be able to:

  • Choose a teaching task that is worth turning into a skill.
  • Write a skill's description so that an AI assistant knows when to use it.
  • Write instructions with ordered steps, checkpoints for the educator's approval, and an exact output.
  • Test the skill on real material and improve it from what went wrong.
  • Run a peer test and record what a colleague needed to use the skill.
  • Submit the skill to the shared library through the GitHub form.

What participants bring

  • A laptop and its charger.
  • An AI assistant account, signed in. Any tool works; testing uses the paste-in method, which works everywhere.
  • A free GitHub account, created before the session (the steps are below). It takes 5 minutes and needs only an email address.
  • Two non-confidential documents for the task you choose: one example of the task done well (your own earlier work), and one fresh input to test the skill on. No patient data and no identifiable learner data.

Before the session (20 minutes, at home)

Send these steps to participants a week before. The GitHub account matters most; creating it in the session costs everyone time.

  1. Create a free GitHub accountGitHub is the website where the library lives. You need an account only to submit your skill through a web form; you will not install anything or write any code. Go to github.com/signup, or open github.com and click Sign up. You can also click Continue with Google.
  2. Enter your email, a password and a usernameUse your academic email so colleagues recognize you. Your username appears publicly next to your submissions; your name and institution are easy choices (for example dana-levi-weizmann). Untick the product-updates box if you prefer.
  3. Pass the verification puzzleGitHub shows a short puzzle to check that you are a person. If it doesn't appear, switch off your ad blocker for the page and reload.
  4. Enter the code from your emailGitHub sends a code to your email address. If it doesn't arrive in a few minutes, check your spam folder. Without a verified email you cannot submit.
  5. Skip the personalization questionsGitHub asks what you plan to use it for. You can skip these; the free plan is all you need. Write down your username and password.
  6. Choose your taskThink of one teaching task you do often and do well, where you know what good looks like: writing objectives for a session, giving feedback on a draft, preparing a journal club, planning a committee meeting. Read the task criteria on this page and bring the two documents.

Preparing as the facilitator

Three weeks before

  • Book a room with power for every seat, a projector and reliable Wi-Fi. Check that the Wi-Fi does not block GitHub or the AI tools.
  • Recruit helpers, one for every 8 participants, who have written and submitted a skill themselves.
  • Tell the library's maintainers the date and the expected number of submissions (see Contact on the About page). First-time submissions are reviewed by a maintainer before they go live, so they can plan to review them in the following days.

One week before

  • Send the invitation with the pre-work, stressing the GitHub account (the email template is below).
  • Choose a published skill to take apart in the session. A short, clear one works best, for example Learning Objectives Designer. Open its SKILL.md on GitHub and read it through.

The day before

  • Write a small skill yourself, start to finish, using the drafting prompt below, and test it with the paste-in method. You will show it as the worked example.
  • Open the new-skill form while signed in, and check the fields against the guide on this page. Don't submit; close the tab.
  • Print, one per participant: the handouts (template, drafting prompt, writing checklist, peer-test sheet and submission guide) from the printable handouts page.
  • Wi-Fi backup: save your worked example, the drafting prompt and the template on your laptop and on paper. Participants can draft offline in any document and submit later from this page.

On the day, 30 minutes before

  • Put the Wi-Fi details, this page's address and the drafting prompt on the board or in a shared document, so participants can copy the prompt.
  • Ask participants as they arrive whether they created their GitHub account. Send those who didn't to a helper to do it straight away, before the session starts.

The session, part by part

For each part: what you do, what participants do, what to say, and how you know the room is ready to move on. Clock times count from the start of the session.

0:00–0:1010 min

Welcome and goals

What we will build, and a skill a colleague already shared. Slides 1–3

You

  • Welcome everyone, introduce the helpers, and show the plan.
  • Show a skill that a colleague contributed to the library, and its page: the author and institution credited, the maturity level, the usage reports.
  • Ask each participant to say their task in one sentence.

Participants

  • Say their task in one sentence to the group or the table.
What to sayBy the end of these two hours, your teaching expertise will be in a form that any colleague, anywhere, can use in their own AI assistant. Not a prompt you retype every time: a skill, tested, and shared with your name and your institution on it.

Ready to move on when: Everyone has a task in one sentence, and a GitHub account (or a helper creating one with them).

0:10–0:2515 min

Anatomy of a skill

Take a published skill apart, and the six-part template. Slides 4–7

You

  • Open the chosen skill's SKILL.md on GitHub (slide 4). Show the parts: the name and description at the top, the steps, the checkpoints, the output, and the limits.
  • Explain the description (slide 5): it is how the assistant decides to use the skill, so it says what the skill does and when, in the words a user would type.
  • Introduce the six-part template (slide 6) that every skill in the library follows, and the common mistakes (slide 7).

Participants

  • Follow along on their own screen, in the same SKILL.md.
What to sayA skill is mostly plain English. The top two lines, name and description, decide whether the assistant ever uses it. The rest reads like a protocol a careful colleague would write for a new team member: what to ask for, what to do in which order, where to stop and check with you, and what the result must look like.

Ready to move on when: Ask: where does the assistant look to decide whether to use a skill? (The description.)

0:25–0:3510 min

Choose your task and write its description

The task criteria, then purpose and description. Slides 8–9

You

  • Go through the criteria for a good first task (slide 8).
  • Ask participants to write the purpose and the description, then read the description to a neighbour.

Participants

  • Write the purpose (one or two sentences) and the description (what it does, when to use it, typical requests).
  • Read the description to a neighbour, who says whether they would know when to use it.
What to sayKeep the first skill small: one task, not a whole job. 'Write learning objectives for one session' is a skill. 'Design my course' is ten skills.

Ready to move on when: Every participant has a purpose and a description that a neighbour understood.

0:35–1:0530 min

Write the skill

Fill the template, draft the instructions, alone or with AI as interviewer. Slides 10–11

You

  • Explain the two ways to draft (slide 10): write it yourself in a document, or let the AI interview you with the drafting prompt.
  • Show your own worked example briefly.
  • Circulate. At 20 minutes, remind everyone that a complete rough draft beats a perfect half: they need every part in place to test it.

Participants

  • Fill in the six parts of the template, then write the instructions: steps in order, checkpoints, the output, the limits.
What to sayWrite down what you actually do, in the order you do it, including the things you check without thinking: those are the expertise. Wherever you would say 'it depends', write a question the skill should ask the educator.

Ready to move on when: Every participant has a complete draft, all six parts and the instructions, by the end of the block.

1:05–1:2015 min

Test and improve

Paste-in test on fresh material, fix the instruction, test again. Slides 12–14

You

  • Explain the test loop (slide 12): new chat, paste the skill, give it the fresh input, compare against the success criteria.
  • Walk through the worked example (slide 13): a failure in the output, the vague line that caused it, the rewritten line, and a rerun on a different input.
  • Stress the rule: when the output is wrong, fix the instruction that caused it, not the output.

Participants

  • Test the skill on the fresh input, fix what went wrong, and run it a second time.
What to sayTesting is where most of the improvement happens. Each thing that goes wrong points at an instruction that was missing or unclear. Fix that line, start a new chat, and run it again.

Ready to move on when: Each skill has run at least twice, with at least one instruction improved.

1:20–1:3515 min

Peer test

A colleague uses your skill without your help. Slides 15

You

  • Explain the peer test (slide 15): swap skills; the colleague uses it on their own material or on your test input; the author watches silently and takes notes.
  • Hand out the peer-test sheet.

Participants

  • Swap skills, use the colleague's skill, and fill in the peer-test sheet for it.
What to sayAuthors: this is the hard part. Don't help. Every time you want to explain something, write it down instead. That's a line missing from your skill.

Ready to move on when: Every skill has a completed peer-test sheet.

1:35–1:5520 min

Share it in the library

The new-skill form on GitHub, field by field. Slides 16–17

You

  • Show the new-skill form on the projector, field by field, using your worked example (slide 16). Don't submit the example.
  • Then let participants submit. Helpers circulate.
  • Explain what happens next (slide 17).

Participants

  • Sign in to GitHub, open the form, copy their parts and instructions into it, tick the rights box, and submit.
What to sayEach field in the form is one of the six parts you've already written, so this is mostly copy and paste. Paste your instructions in the Instructions field. Tick the credit box if you want your name on the skill; your institution is recorded either way.

Ready to move on when: Every participant who is ready has submitted. The others know how to submit later.

1:55–2:005 min

Closing

What happens next, and how the skill grows. Slides 18

You

  • Go around: one thing each participant learned about their own expertise by writing it down.
  • Close with the next steps: watch for the reply on their submission, ask colleagues to send usage reports, and review someone else's skill.

Participants

  • Say one thing they learned.
What to sayYour skill starts as a Draft. Every colleague who uses it and reports back moves it up. Thank you for sharing what you know.

Ready to move on when: Everyone knows where to find their submission.

Handouts and step-by-step guides

Each is written for someone doing it for the first time. To print only these, use the printable handouts page.

Is this task a good first skill?

  • You do it often, so a skill saves you and others real time.
  • You do it well and know what a good result looks like.
  • It is one task, with a clear start and a clear result, not a whole job.
  • Other educators do it too, at other institutions.
  • It needs no confidential data: no patient data and no identifiable learner data.

The six-part template

Every skill in the library is described in the same six parts. They are also the fields of the submission form.

  1. PurposeWhat task the skill does, and when an educator should use it. One or two sentences.
  2. InputsWhat the user must provide (a syllabus, a paper, a list of objectives), and what the skill should ask for if it is missing.
  3. InstructionsThe steps the AI follows, in order, the standards it applies, and the points where it stops for the educator's approval.
  4. Expected outputThe exact shape of the result: its sections, format and length.
  5. Success criteriaHow you will know the output is good enough to use.
  6. LimitationsWhat the skill does not do, where it may fail, and what the educator must always check.

What a SKILL.md looks like

A skill is a text file named SKILL.md. The two lines between the dashes at the top are read first; the rest is read when the skill is used. You don't have to build this file: the submission form builds it from what you paste. It helps to see it.

---
name: journal-club-feedback
description: Gives structured feedback on a trainee's journal club presentation plan. Use when an educator asks to review, check or improve a journal club plan or slides.
---

# Journal Club Feedback

## Step 1: Gather what you need
Ask for the paper and the trainee's plan if they are missing.
Ask the trainee's stage (first year, senior) if it isn't stated.

## Step 2: Check the plan against the standards
...

## Step 3: Stop for approval
Show the three main points of feedback. Wait for the educator to approve or edit them.

## Output
A one-page feedback note: strengths, three changes in priority order, one question for the trainee.

## Limitations
Does not judge the science of the paper. Never invents references.

The drafting prompt

Copy this into a new chat in your AI assistant, fill in your task, and answer its questions one at a time. Read the draft it writes carefully; it is your expertise, so correct anything it got wrong.

I am an educator. I want to turn one teaching task that I do well into an AI skill that colleagues at other institutions can use in their own AI assistant.

The task: [describe it in one sentence]

Interview me one question at a time. Find out: when the task is needed; what material I start from; the steps I follow, in order; the standards I apply and the common mistakes; where a human must check or approve before going on; the exact shape of the result; how I judge whether it is good; and what the skill must not do.

When you have enough, write the skill as a SKILL.md file: a header with a name (lowercase words joined by hyphens) and a description that says what the skill does and when to use it, in words a user would type. Then numbered steps with the checkpoints, the output format, the success criteria and the limitations.

Do not invent standards, rules or references. Where you are unsure, ask me. Keep it in plain English and under two pages.

Writing checklist

  • The description says what and when, in words a user would type.
  • One task. If it has two results, it is probably two skills.
  • Steps in order, one action each, numbered.
  • Asks instead of guessing when input is missing.
  • Stops for approval wherever judgement matters.
  • The output is exact: sections, format, length.
  • Standards are named, with their source. It never invents references.
  • Limits are stated, including the data rule: no patient data, no identifiable learner data.
  • Plain language. Explain local terms, so the skill works at another institution.

The test loop

  1. Start a new chatA clean chat, so nothing from your drafting conversation leaks in.
  2. Paste the whole skill as your first messageThen, below it: "Follow the skill above for my request." This paste-in test works in every AI tool.
  3. Give it the fresh inputYour second document, not the example the skill was written from, and ask in the words a colleague would use.
  4. Judge against your success criteriaDid it ask for what it needed? Stop where it should? Produce the exact output? Invent anything?
  5. Fix the instruction, not the outputFor each problem, change or add the line in the skill that would have prevented it. Then start again from step 1.

Peer-test sheet

Filled in by the colleague who tests the skill. The author watches without helping.

  • Skill tested, and its author:
  • Could you use it without the author's help? yes / with help / no
  • Where did you hesitate, or what did you need to ask?
  • Did the skill ask for what it needed? yes / partly / no
  • Was the output usable? as it was / after edits / no
  • Did anything look invented or wrong?
  • One change that would make it easier for the next educator:

Submit your skill, field by field

Sign in to GitHub, then open the new-skill form. It is also linked from the Contribute page. Each field matches a part you have already written.

  1. About youYour name and role (optional), Institution and Country (required). Tick Credit me by name if you want your name on the skill's page and the community page; your institution is recorded either way.
  2. Name and purposeProposed name: a few words, for example journal-club-feedback. Purpose: what the skill does and when to use it.
  3. Who uses it, and how they askTick who uses the skill (educators, learners, program staff). Under Tasks, write a few requests someone might type, one per line.
  4. The six partsCopy in Inputs, Expected output, Success criteria and Limitations from your template.
  5. InstructionsPaste your whole SKILL.md, or your instructions, into Instructions. If it is very long, save it as a text file or a ZIP and drag it into the field.
  6. Testing and rightsSay whether you have used it for real teaching, and in which AI tools. Tick the Rights box: you confirm that you may share it and that it contains no confidential data.
  7. SubmitClick Create (or Submit new issue). Your submission opens as a page on GitHub. Keep its address.

What happens after you submit

  1. A robot builds and checks itWithin a few minutes, the library's automation turns your form into a skill and checks that it is complete. If something is missing, it says so in a comment on your submission; edit the submission to fix it.
  2. A maintainer reviews your first skillThe first skill from a new contributor is reviewed by a maintainer before it goes live. You get a comment, and an email from GitHub, when it is published or if anything needs a change. After your first accepted skill, later ones publish automatically.
  3. It appears as a DraftYour skill gets its own page in the catalog, with your institution (and your name, if you ticked the box) credited. Draft means only its author has tested it.
  4. It grows with useAsk colleagues to use it and send a usage report. Each report from another institution moves it up, towards Tested elsewhere and Reviewed.

When something goes wrong

The assistant ignores one of my steps.

Number the steps and make each one a single action. Move the ignored step earlier, or state it as a rule ("Always…", "Never…").

The output is generic.

Add the standards you apply and a short example of a good result. Generic output usually means the expertise is still in your head, not in the skill.

It invents references or facts.

Add a rule: "Never invent sources. Where something can't be verified from the material, mark it [check] and tell the educator."

My colleague couldn't use it.

That is a useful result. Look at what they asked you; usually the inputs or the description were unclear. Fix those lines.

I can't sign in to GitHub, or the form doesn't open.

Check the code in your email (and spam folder) to verify the account. If it still fails, email your skill and the six parts to the founders (Contact, on the About page) and they will submit it with you as the author.

The form says my text is too long.

Save the instructions as a text file or a ZIP and drag it into the Instructions field instead of pasting.

The Wi-Fi fails.

Keep drafting offline in a document; the template and checklist are on paper. Test in pairs on any device that still connects, and submit after the session with the submission guide.

After the session

  • Within 48 hours, email participants the link to their submissions and to the usage report form, so they can ask colleagues to try their skills while the session is fresh.
  • Watch the submissions from your session on GitHub, and help participants answer any comments.
  • A week later, remind participants to ask one colleague each to use their skill and send a usage report.
  • For a conference or a shorter slot, the 75-minute round-table version builds skills by area of work at five tables.
