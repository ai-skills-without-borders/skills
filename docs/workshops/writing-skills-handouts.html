Handouts: Writing AI skills

Everything participants need on paper, in one page. Print it from your browser, or share the link.

The full plan

Before the session (20 minutes, at home)

  1. Create a free GitHub accountGitHub is the website where the library lives. You need an account only to submit your skill through a web form; you will not install anything or write any code. Go to github.com/signup, or open github.com and click Sign up. You can also click Continue with Google.
  2. Enter your email, a password and a usernameUse your academic email so colleagues recognize you. Your username appears publicly next to your submissions; your name and institution are easy choices (for example dana-levi-weizmann). Untick the product-updates box if you prefer.
  3. Pass the verification puzzleGitHub shows a short puzzle to check that you are a person. If it doesn't appear, switch off your ad blocker for the page and reload.
  4. Enter the code from your emailGitHub sends a code to your email address. If it doesn't arrive in a few minutes, check your spam folder. Without a verified email you cannot submit.
  5. Skip the personalization questionsGitHub asks what you plan to use it for. You can skip these; the free plan is all you need. Write down your username and password.
  6. Choose your taskThink of one teaching task you do often and do well, where you know what good looks like: writing objectives for a session, giving feedback on a draft, preparing a journal club, planning a committee meeting. Read the task criteria on this page and bring the two documents.

Is this task a good first skill?

  • You do it often, so a skill saves you and others real time.
  • You do it well and know what a good result looks like.
  • It is one task, with a clear start and a clear result, not a whole job.
  • Other educators do it too, at other institutions.
  • It needs no confidential data: no patient data and no identifiable learner data.

The six-part template

Every skill in the library is described in the same six parts. They are also the fields of the submission form.

  1. PurposeWhat task the skill does, and when an educator should use it. One or two sentences.
  2. InputsWhat the user must provide (a syllabus, a paper, a list of objectives), and what the skill should ask for if it is missing.
  3. InstructionsThe steps the AI follows, in order, the standards it applies, and the points where it stops for the educator's approval.
  4. Expected outputThe exact shape of the result: its sections, format and length.
  5. Success criteriaHow you will know the output is good enough to use.
  6. LimitationsWhat the skill does not do, where it may fail, and what the educator must always check.

What a SKILL.md looks like

A skill is a text file named SKILL.md. The two lines between the dashes at the top are read first; the rest is read when the skill is used. You don't have to build this file: the submission form builds it from what you paste. It helps to see it.

---
name: journal-club-feedback
description: Gives structured feedback on a trainee's journal club presentation plan. Use when an educator asks to review, check or improve a journal club plan or slides.
---

# Journal Club Feedback

## Step 1: Gather what you need
Ask for the paper and the trainee's plan if they are missing.
Ask the trainee's stage (first year, senior) if it isn't stated.

## Step 2: Check the plan against the standards
...

## Step 3: Stop for approval
Show the three main points of feedback. Wait for the educator to approve or edit them.

## Output
A one-page feedback note: strengths, three changes in priority order, one question for the trainee.

## Limitations
Does not judge the science of the paper. Never invents references.

The drafting prompt

Copy this into a new chat in your AI assistant, fill in your task, and answer its questions one at a time. Read the draft it writes carefully; it is your expertise, so correct anything it got wrong.

I am an educator. I want to turn one teaching task that I do well into an AI skill that colleagues at other institutions can use in their own AI assistant.

The task: [describe it in one sentence]

Interview me one question at a time. Find out: when the task is needed; what material I start from; the steps I follow, in order; the standards I apply and the common mistakes; where a human must check or approve before going on; the exact shape of the result; how I judge whether it is good; and what the skill must not do.

When you have enough, write the skill as a SKILL.md file: a header with a name (lowercase words joined by hyphens) and a description that says what the skill does and when to use it, in words a user would type. Then numbered steps with the checkpoints, the output format, the success criteria and the limitations.

Do not invent standards, rules or references. Where you are unsure, ask me. Keep it in plain English and under two pages.

Writing checklist

  • The description says what and when, in words a user would type.
  • One task. If it has two results, it is probably two skills.
  • Steps in order, one action each, numbered.
  • Asks instead of guessing when input is missing.
  • Stops for approval wherever judgement matters.
  • The output is exact: sections, format, length.
  • Standards are named, with their source. It never invents references.
  • Limits are stated, including the data rule: no patient data, no identifiable learner data.
  • Plain language. Explain local terms, so the skill works at another institution.

The test loop

  1. Start a new chatA clean chat, so nothing from your drafting conversation leaks in.
  2. Paste the whole skill as your first messageThen, below it: "Follow the skill above for my request." This paste-in test works in every AI tool.
  3. Give it the fresh inputYour second document, not the example the skill was written from, and ask in the words a colleague would use.
  4. Judge against your success criteriaDid it ask for what it needed? Stop where it should? Produce the exact output? Invent anything?
  5. Fix the instruction, not the outputFor each problem, change or add the line in the skill that would have prevented it. Then start again from step 1.

Peer-test sheet

Filled in by the colleague who tests the skill. The author watches without helping.

  • Skill tested, and its author:
  • Could you use it without the author's help? yes / with help / no
  • Where did you hesitate, or what did you need to ask?
  • Did the skill ask for what it needed? yes / partly / no
  • Was the output usable? as it was / after edits / no
  • Did anything look invented or wrong?
  • One change that would make it easier for the next educator:

Submit your skill, field by field

Sign in to GitHub, then open the new-skill form. It is also linked from the Contribute page. Each field matches a part you have already written.

  1. About youYour name and role (optional), Institution and Country (required). Tick Credit me by name if you want your name on the skill's page and the community page; your institution is recorded either way.
  2. Name and purposeProposed name: a few words, for example journal-club-feedback. Purpose: what the skill does and when to use it.
  3. Who uses it, and how they askTick who uses the skill (educators, learners, program staff). Under Tasks, write a few requests someone might type, one per line.
  4. The six partsCopy in Inputs, Expected output, Success criteria and Limitations from your template.
  5. InstructionsPaste your whole SKILL.md, or your instructions, into Instructions. If it is very long, save it as a text file or a ZIP and drag it into the field.
  6. Testing and rightsSay whether you have used it for real teaching, and in which AI tools. Tick the Rights box: you confirm that you may share it and that it contains no confidential data.
  7. SubmitClick Create (or Submit new issue). Your submission opens as a page on GitHub. Keep its address.

What happens after you submit

  1. A robot builds and checks itWithin a few minutes, the library's automation turns your form into a skill and checks that it is complete. If something is missing, it says so in a comment on your submission; edit the submission to fix it.
  2. A maintainer reviews your first skillThe first skill from a new contributor is reviewed by a maintainer before it goes live. You get a comment, and an email from GitHub, when it is published or if anything needs a change. After your first accepted skill, later ones publish automatically.
  3. It appears as a DraftYour skill gets its own page in the catalog, with your institution (and your name, if you ticked the box) credited. Draft means only its author has tested it.
  4. It grows with useAsk colleagues to use it and send a usage report. Each report from another institution moves it up, towards Tested elsewhere and Reviewed.
