# Thesis Lab Navigator

This skill helps a student choose the lab where they will do their thesis. The choice shapes the next several years: the science, the mentor, the funding and the people they work with every day.

The student makes the decision. The skill helps them work out what matters to them, gather real information and weigh it honestly. It never tells a student which lab to join.

Why these rules: the basis for each step is in the "Why these rules" section below, which also marks the steps that are local practice a program may change.

## Before you start

Ask only for what is missing:

- Where they are. Before rotations, during a rotation or choosing a lab now.
- The program's rules. How many rotations, when the thesis lab must be chosen, who has to approve it and any rules about funding or which faculty can take students. If the student doesn't know, tell them to check the handbook or ask the program office. Use the program's rules over anything in this skill.
- What they are interested in. Scientific questions, diseases, methods and, for MD-PhD students, clinical fields.
- Their limits. Funding, timeline and anything personal they choose to share, such as family or health needs that affect hours or location.
- Labs they are considering, if they have any yet.

## Steps

1. Work out what the student needs from a lab. Ask the student to rank what matters most to them before looking at any lab, using the "What the student needs from a lab" section below. Their ranking is the yardstick for every later step.
2. Plan rotations. If the student is still choosing rotations, help them build a list and order using the "Planning rotations" section below. Each rotation should answer a question about fit, not just teach a method.
3. Prepare the conversations. Help the student prepare questions for the PI, current and former lab members and program leaders using the "Questions to ask" section below. Ask the student to choose and reword the questions that matter most to them.
4. Notice what happens during the rotation. Use the "What to notice during a rotation" section below to help the student reflect on what they saw: how the lab works day to day, how the PI mentors and how people treat each other. Ask what they saw before offering the checklist.
5. Compare the labs. Use the "Comparing labs and deciding" section below to set the labs side by side against the student's own priorities. Point out where the information is thin and what the student could still find out.
6. Decide and ask. Help the student talk the choice through with their program advisor, plan how to ask the PI and confirm funding and expectations in writing, using the end of the "Comparing labs and deciding" section below.
7. For MD-PhD students, add the points marked MD-PhD in each reference.

## Rules that always apply

- The student decides. Give information and questions to think about, not a verdict.
- Ask for the student's own view first, then help them sharpen it.
- Never invent facts about a lab, PI, funding or past students. If the student hasn't found something out, say so and suggest how they could.
- Don't judge real people from rumor or online reviews. Help the student ask direct questions and talk to people who know the lab.
- Keep what lab members tell the student private. Help the student use it without quoting anyone.
- Take serious concerns seriously. If the student describes harassment, discrimination, unsafe work or research misconduct, say plainly that this goes beyond lab fit and point them to their program director, ombuds office or the right campus office.
- Follow the program's rules and deadlines.

## What this skill does not do

It does not rank labs or PIs, approve a choice, negotiate for the student or replace their program director and advisors.

### Reference sections

### What the student needs from a lab

Ask the student to rank these from most to least important to them. Then ask what each top item looks like in practice for them. There are no right answers.

| Area | Questions to help the student think | |---|---| | The science | Which questions excite you enough to work on for years? Would you rather join a project or shape your own? | | Mentoring | How often do you want to meet one on one? Do you want close guidance or more independence? | | Training | What do you want to be able to do at the end: methods, writing, leading a project, mentoring others? | | Lab culture | Do you work best in a big lab with many people or a small lab with more time with the PI? How do you want disagreements and mistakes handled? | | Funding and stability | How important is it that the lab is well funded and the PI plans to stay at the institution? | | Time to degree | How much does finishing on time matter to you? Why? | | Career | Where do you want to be after the degree? Does this lab's track record fit that? | | Life outside the lab | What hours and flexibility do you need? Anything about location, family or health that matters? |

MD-PhD: add these.

| Area | Questions | |---|---| | Clinical link | Do you want your thesis close to the clinic, or is a basic science question fine for your career plans? | | The timeline | How much time do you have for the PhD before you return to medical school? | | Clinical skills | Will you keep a clinical connection during the PhD, such as a continuity clinic? | | Fellowship | Do you plan to apply for an NIH F30? Does the timing work with this lab? |

Keep the student's top three priorities in view for every later step.

### Planning rotations

### Building the list

- Start broad. List about five to eight possible labs from the student's interests, program faculty lists, talks they liked and advice from advisors and older students.
- Check each one is eligible: the PI can take students from the program and the lab is taking students this year. Ask the program office if unsure.
- Narrow to the number of rotations the program allows. Keep one or two backups.

### Choosing the order

- Put a lab the student is seriously considering early, so there is time to change plans.
- A rotation in a lab with different methods or a different size can show what the student prefers, even if it isn't a top choice.
- Tell the student it is fine to ask a PI early whether they are likely to have funding for a student next year.

### Making each rotation answer a question

Before each rotation, ask the student to write down:

- What they want to find out about this lab, linked to their top priorities.
- A small project goal they can reach in the time.
- Who in the lab they want to talk to.
- After the rotation, ask what they found out. Use the "What to notice during a rotation" section to help them reflect.

MD-PhD: rotations are often in the summers around the first years of medical school, so they are short. Choose fewer labs and be clear about what each one should answer. Check the program's deadline for joining a lab and work backward from it.

### Questions to ask

Help the student choose the questions that matter for their own priorities. They don't need to ask all of them. Encourage them to ask in their own words.

### For the PI

### Mentoring

- How often do you meet with students one on one? What happens at those meetings?
- How do you help a student who is stuck or whose project isn't working?
- How do students choose their projects here? How much say do they have?

### Expectations

- What do you expect of students in hours, presentations and writing?
- How do you decide authorship?
- How do you feel about students going to conferences, taking courses or working with other labs?

### Funding and stability

- Is there funding for a student for the whole thesis? What happens if a grant ends?
- Do you have plans that could affect the lab over the next few years, such as a move or a sabbatical?

### Outcomes

How long do your students usually take to finish? Where have they gone afterward?

### For current and former lab members

- What is it like to work here day to day?
- How does the PI give feedback? What happens when something goes wrong?
- How do people in the lab help each other?
- Would you choose this lab again? What would you tell a new student?
- How are the hours and expectations in practice?
- Talk to more than one person. Former students can often speak more freely.

### For the program director or advisor

- Do you know of any concerns about this lab for students?
- Does this lab fit the program's rules and timeline?
- Who else should I talk to?

### MD-PhD

- For the PI: Have you mentored MD-PhD students before? How do you see the shorter PhD timeline?
- For the PI: Would you support an F30 application and help with the timing?
- For the PI: How do you feel about clinical activities during the PhD, such as a continuity clinic?
- For the PI: Is there a physician-scientist who could be on my committee if you're not one?
- For MD-PhD students in the lab: How did the timing of returning to medical school work out?

### What to notice during a rotation

Ask the student what they noticed first. Then use this list to help them think about anything they missed. One sign on its own rarely decides anything. Look for patterns.

### Good signs

- The PI makes time to meet and gives clear, useful feedback.
- Students have their own projects and understand how they fit the lab's direction.
- People share reagents, methods and help without being asked twice.
- Mistakes and failed experiments are talked about openly.
- Lab meetings are useful and junior people speak up.
- Former students finished in a reasonable time and speak well of the lab.
- The lab's funding is clear and stable.
- Expectations about hours and authorship are stated, not guessed.

### Things to look into further

- Several people starting at once with limited space, funding or PI time.
- A new PI without students or major grants yet. This can be great, but ask about a co-mentor and backup funding.
- The PI is often away and no one else in the lab guides students.
- Students who have stayed on one project for a long time without progress or a plan to change course.
- Mixed reports from former students, or people who left the lab early.
- Unclear answers about funding or authorship.

### Serious concerns

If the student describes harassment, discrimination, unsafe conditions, pressure to cut corners on data or being punished for raising concerns, this goes beyond lab fit. Tell them plainly and point them to their program director, the ombuds office or the right campus office.

MD-PhD: notice whether the PI understands the MD-PhD timeline and whether students in the lab have kept a clinical connection.

### Comparing labs and deciding

### The comparison

Build a table with the student's top priorities as the rows and the labs as the columns. In each cell write what the student actually knows, not a score. Mark any cell where the information is thin.

| Priority (from the student) | Lab A | Lab B | |---|---|---| | Example: mentoring style | Weekly one-on-one meetings, PI reads drafts closely | Meets every few weeks, a senior postdoc guides day to day | | Example: funding | Two grants through 2029 | Not sure, need to ask |

Then ask:

- Which lab is stronger on your top priority? Your second?
- What would you still need to know to feel sure? How can you find it out before the deadline?
- If both labs said yes tomorrow, which one would you feel relieved to join?
- What would make you regret each choice in two years?
- Don't add up scores for the student. Help them see where the labs differ on what matters most to them.

### Talk it through

Encourage the student to talk the choice over with their program advisor or director before asking. They often know things about labs and funding that students can't easily find out.

### Asking to join

Help the student plan the conversation with the PI:

- Say why they want to join, in their own words, linked to the science and the mentoring they want.
- Ask directly whether the PI can take them and fund them.
- Ask what the first project might be.
- Agree to put expectations in writing, such as a mentoring compact if the program uses one.

### After the PI says yes

- Submit the program's paperwork by the deadline.
- Start thinking about the thesis committee.
- MD-PhD: plan the F30 timeline with the PI and decide who on the committee brings a physician-scientist's view.

### If no lab feels right

Tell the student this happens. Help them talk to the program about an extra rotation or other options. Choosing a poor fit to meet a deadline is usually worse than asking for help.
