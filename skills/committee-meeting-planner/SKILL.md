# Committee Meeting Planner

This skill helps a graduate student organize their own thesis committee meeting. It covers checking when the meeting is due, writing to the committee, following up on the poll, sending the invite, preparing the slides and progress report and writing up what the committee advised. It works for PhD, MD-PhD and other graduate students, because each student gives it their program's rules.

Students often find this part of training stressful: writing to busy senior faculty, chasing replies and deciding what to present. The skill's job is to make it simple and to help the student get real advice out of the meeting.

Why these rules: the basis for each step is in `references/pedagogical-basis.md`, which also marks the steps that are local practice a program may change.

## Before you start

Ask only for what is missing:

- The program's rules. How often committees meet, who must attend, how long the meeting runs and what to send ahead. If the student doesn't know, use the defaults in the "Program rules" section below and suggest they check the program handbook or ask the program coordinator.
- The committee. The mentor, the chair and the other members, with email addresses. Ask whether anyone is outside the institution or in another time zone.
- The last meeting. Its date and what the committee asked for, if there was one.
- The mentor. Ask whether the mentor has agreed it's time for a meeting. If not, suggest the student check with them before writing to the committee.
- What the AI tool can do. Ask whether it can see the student's calendar or send email. If it can, still draft everything first and send nothing until the student approves. If it can't, produce text the student copies.

## Steps

1. Check when the meeting is due. Use the program's rule and the last meeting date. If the student is overdue, say so kindly and suggest scheduling within the next month.
2. Draft the poll email. Use the template in the "Emails, invite and agenda" section below. Offer 8 to 12 time slots over two to four weeks, all with time zones. Copy the mentor and, if the program wants, the program coordinator. Stop and show the draft for approval.
3. Follow up on the poll. When the student comes back with responses, follow the poll steps in the "Emails, invite and agenda" section below: send polite personal reminders, pick the best time and handle the case where no time works. Stop for approval before each email.
4. Book a room if needed. Remind the student to book a room and set up a video link before sending the invite.
5. Send the calendar invite. Build it from the "Emails, invite and agenda" section below. If the tool can create calendar events, create it only after the student approves. Otherwise give the student the invite text to paste.
6. Prepare the slides. Help the student outline their talk using the "What to put in your slides" section below. Pick the version that fits: first meeting, regular meeting or pre-defense meeting. Spend the most time on the slide of questions for the committee.
7. Write the progress report. Help draft it using the "Progress report" section below, from the student's own notes and data. Suggest the student share the slides and report with their mentor a few days before they go to the committee.
8. Send materials and reminders. One week before, send the committee the report and slides. One day before, send a short reminder with the place or link.
9. Plan the agenda. Use the agenda in the "Emails, invite and agenda" section below. Check it with the chair if the program asks for that.
10. Write up the meeting. After the meeting, turn the student's notes into a summary using the "Writing up the meeting" section below. The student sends it to the committee to confirm it matches what they said. Stop for approval before it goes to anyone.

Steps 2 to 10 often happen weeks apart. When the student comes back, ask where they are and pick up from that step.

## Rules that always apply

- Never send an email, invite or summary without the student's approval.
- Use only what the student gives. Never guess an email address, a date, a result or what a committee member said.
- Help the student present their work honestly. Never invent data, overstate results or hide experiments that failed.
- Write emails the way a respectful, organized student writes to faculty: short, clear and polite, without over-apologizing. Always state the time zone.
- Keep private things private. If the student mentions health, family or other personal matters, leave them out of anything sent to the committee unless the student says otherwise. Suggest they talk with their mentor or program director directly.
- If the program's rules and the student's plan conflict, point it out and suggest the student check with the program coordinator.

## What this skill does not do

It does not judge the student's science, decide whether they are making enough progress or replace the committee's own report form, which the chair usually completes.

### Emails, invite and agenda

These are written from the student to the committee. Fill in everything in brackets. Always write out the time zone. Keep emails short and polite.

### Poll email to the committee

Subject: Thesis committee meeting for [your name]: please choose times by [date]

Dear Drs. [names],

I'm due for my next thesis committee meeting and would like to hold it in [month]. Could you please mark every time that works for you in this poll by [date, about five working days away]? [poll link]

The meeting will run [90 minutes] [in person in room / on video / in person with a video link]. All times are [time zone].

Thank you, [your name]

Copy your mentor. Offer 8 to 12 slots over two to four weeks. Avoid holidays and the start of the semester if possible. If anyone is in another time zone, choose times in their working hours and show both time zones.

### Following up on the poll

### After the deadline, or about five working days

Send each person who hasn't answered a short personal email, not a reply to everyone:

Dear Dr. [name],

I wanted to follow up on the poll for my committee meeting. If you're able to mark your times by [new date, two to three days away], I'll set the date then: [poll link]

Thank you, [your name]

If someone still hasn't answered two days later, ask your mentor whether they can reach out, or ask the person's assistant.

### Picking the time

- Keep only times when the chair, your mentor and you can all attend.
- Among those, choose the time with the most other members.
- If two times tie, choose the earlier one, so you have more time afterward to act on the committee's advice.
- Check that the result meets the program's quorum.

### When no time works

- If one member can't make any time, ask whether they can join by video or send comments ahead.
- If the chair or your mentor can't make any time, send a second poll with new dates two to four weeks later.
- Let the program coordinator know if the delay will make you overdue.

### Confirming the time

Subject: Confirmed: my committee meeting on [day, date, time, time zone]

Dear all,

Thank you for filling out the poll. My committee meeting will be on [day, date] from [start] to [end] [time zone] in [room] / on [video link]. I'll send a calendar invitation shortly. My progress report and slides will follow by [date].

[If someone can't attend:] Dr. [name] isn't able to join at this time and will [send comments ahead / join for part of the meeting].

Best, [your name]

### Sending materials, one week before

### Reminder, one day before

Subject: Tomorrow: my committee meeting

Dear all, a reminder that my committee meeting is tomorrow, [time, time zone], in [place / link]. I look forward to seeing you.

### Agenda for a 90-minute meeting

Adjust the times to the program's meeting length.

| Time | Item |
|---|---|
| 10 min | Committee meets without you, if the program does this |
| 5 min | Chair opens and reviews the action items from last time |
| 30 min | You present |
| 30 min | Discussion and advice |
| 10 min | Your training and career plans |
| 5 min | Chair sums up decisions, action items and the next meeting date |

### What to put in your slides

Plan on about 30 minutes of talking, which is usually 15 to 20 slides. Your committee wants to see your progress, your problems and your plan. Above all, the meeting is your chance to get advice from several experts at once, so come with specific questions.

### Regular meeting

- Title slide. Your name, the date, your mentor and committee members.
- Since last time. The committee's advice and action items from the last meeting, with what happened to each one.
- The big question. One slide with the main question of your thesis and your aims.
- Progress on each aim. For each aim, your key data. Give each slide a title that says what the figure shows, for example "Knocking out X reduces viral replication by half". Show the number of samples and the statistics on the slide.
- What didn't work. Experiments that failed or changed direction, with what you learned. Committees expect these. They can often help.
- Your plan. What comes next for each aim over the next six months, with a simple timeline.
- Questions for the committee. Two or three specific decisions or problems you want advice on. For example, "Should I add a second mouse model or go straight to patient samples?" This is the most useful slide in the meeting.
- Training and career. Goals from your development plan, papers and abstracts, fellowship applications, courses and conferences. MD-PhD students add their plan and timing for returning to clinical training.
- Backup slides. Extra data the committee might ask about.

### First meeting

Replace slides 2, 4 and 5 with:

- Background. What is known and the gap your thesis will fill.
- Aims and approach. For each aim, the question, your approach and what each possible result would mean.
- Preliminary data. Anything that shows the approach can work.
- Risks. What could go wrong and your backup plan.

### Pre-defense meeting

Add:

- Thesis outline. The chapters and which papers or manuscripts each one comes from.
- What's left. The remaining experiments and writing, with dates.
- Proposed defense date.

### Tips

- Show your mentor the slides a few days before you send them to the committee.
- Make every figure readable from the back of the room.
- Practice once with your lab so the talk fits in 30 minutes, leaving the rest for discussion.
- The Lecture Slide Reviewer skill can review your slides before they go out.

### Progress report

Most programs ask for a short written report a week before the meeting, usually 2 to 3 pages. It lets the committee come ready to give advice. Write it from the student's own notes and data. Never add results that aren't there.

### Outline

Thesis title and aims. The working title and each aim in one sentence.

Since the last meeting. A short list of the committee's last action items and what happened with each one.

Progress on each aim. For each aim, a paragraph on what was done and found, with one or two key figures. Say plainly what is finished, what is in progress and what didn't work.

Plan for the next six months. What you will do for each aim, in order, with rough dates.

Questions for the committee. The same two or three questions as on the slides.

Training and career. Papers, abstracts and talks, fellowship applications, courses, conferences and goals from your development plan. MD-PhD students add their timeline for returning to clinical training.

### Tips

- Keep it to the page limit. Committees read short reports more carefully.
- Use the same figure titles and aim names as the slides, so the two match.
- Share it with your mentor before it goes to the committee.

### Writing up the meeting

Soon after the meeting, while it's fresh, the student writes a short summary of the committee's advice and sends it to the committee to confirm. This makes sure everyone agrees on what was decided. It also becomes the starting point for the next meeting.

Write it from the student's notes. Use only what the student gives. If something is unclear, such as who agreed to do what, ask.

Keep it to one page.

Subject: Summary of my committee meeting on [date]

Dear all,

Thank you for your time and advice at my committee meeting on [date]. Here is my summary. Please let me know if I've missed or misunderstood anything.

Present: [names]. Absent: [names].

What I presented: two or three sentences.

### Your advice

one line per point

Decisions: anything decided, such as dropping an aim, adding an experiment or approving a defense timeline.

| What | Who | By when |
|---|---|---|

Next meeting: due by [date].

Thank you again, [your name]

### Tips

- Send it within a few days of the meeting.
