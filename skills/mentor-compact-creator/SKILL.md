# Mentor Compact Creator

A mentoring compact is a short written agreement about what a mentor and a trainee expect from each other. Most mentoring problems come from expectations that were never said out loud, like how often to meet, who decides authorship or how much time off is normal. A compact puts those expectations on paper early, when they are easy to talk about.

This skill builds a compact in two layers:

- The program layer. What the program expects of every trainee and mentor: shared values, responsibilities, milestones, funding, safety, time away and who to contact. The program writes this once and every pair uses the same version.
- The pair layer. What this mentor and this trainee agree on for themselves, within the program's rules. Every pair writes their own.
- The skill can do either layer or both.

Why these rules: the basis for each step is in `references/pedagogical-basis.md`, which also marks the steps that are local practice a program may change.

## Before you start

Ask only for what is missing:

- Who is using it. A program director setting up the program layer, or a trainee or mentor working on their pair layer.
- The program's existing compact or policies, if any. A current compact, handbook, milestone document or policy pages. Program-specific details like dates, funding rules and form links come only from these, never from the skill.
- The trainee's stage. For example graduate student, MD-PhD student in the research years or postdoc.
- New or update. For an update, ask for the current compact.

## Setting up the program layer

- Start from what exists. If the program has a compact, sort its content into the sections in the "Program layer template" section below. If it has none, use that template and ask the program director to fill in each section from their policies.
- Keep policy and personal choices apart. Anything that applies to every pair, like vacation allowance or milestone deadlines, goes in the program layer. Anything each pair should decide, like meeting frequency, goes in the pair layer. Flag anything that could go either way and ask.
- Mark what pairs may change. For each program section, note whether pairs can add detail, like more vacation by mutual agreement, or must follow it as written, like the dissertation deposit deadline.
- Check it reads well. Plain language, short paragraphs, dates and deadlines in a table, no undefined abbreviations and every form or handbook mentioned by name with a link.
- Stop for approval. The program director approves the program layer before anyone uses it.

## Writing the pair layer

- Send the same questions to both people. Use the "Questions for both people" section below, leaving out anything the program layer already settles. Each person answers on their own.
- Compare the answers. Sort each topic into "we agree", "small difference" or "needs a conversation" using the "Comparing the answers" section below.
- Prepare the conversation. For each topic that needs a conversation, write one or two neutral questions for the pair to talk through, ideally in person.
- Draft the pair layer. After they talk, write their agreements using the "Pair layer template" section below. It sits after the program layer, in the space many compacts leave for notes or amendments.
- Stop for review. Both people read the draft and suggest changes. Revise until both are satisfied.
- Set a review date. Usually every 6 to 12 months, or when something big changes, like a qualifying exam, a new funding source or the return to clinical training.

## Rules that always apply

- The pair layer can add detail to the program layer but never contradict it. If a pair wants something that conflicts, point it out and suggest they check with the program.
- Program details come only from the program. Never invent a date, policy, dollar amount or form.
- The pair layer reflects what both people agreed. Never put words in one person's mouth.
- Keep the pair layer to about one page.
- Keep private things private. Include health, family or other personal matters only if the person explicitly wants them written down. Accommodations go through the institution's official process.
- A compact is a shared understanding, not a legal contract. Say so in the document.
- If the relationship is in serious trouble, suggest the trainee talk with the program director or an ombudsperson.

## What this skill does not do

It does not judge whether a mentor or trainee is doing a good job, settle disputes or replace institutional policies.

### Reference sections

### Program layer template

The program fills this in once. Use the program's own wording and details. Leave out any section that doesn't apply. For each section, mark whether pairs may add detail ("Pairs may add") or must follow it as written ("Program rule").

### Welcome and purpose

Two or three sentences: what the compact is for, that every mentoring relationship is different and that the student and mentor should review it together and come back to it as things change.

### Shared values

The kind of training environment the program expects: rigorous, ethical, respectful, inclusive and free of discrimination, harassment and retaliation. Mention any lab code of conduct the program encourages.

### Student responsibilities

Managing their own progress through the program and meeting milestones and deadlines

### Keeping complete, accurate and timely research records

### Presenting raw and analyzed data to the mentor regularly

### Following lab, research integrity and safety policies

### Taking part in required program activities

### Mentor responsibilities

Scientific training, including rigor, reproducibility and responsible conduct of research

### A safe, ethical and inclusive lab environment

- Clear expectations on communication, lab culture, authorship, data, schedules and conduct
- Regular meetings, timely feedback and growing independence for the trainee
- Help developing writing and speaking through presentations, papers and fellowship applications

### Support for required program activities and timely completion

For dual-degree programs: support for the transition back to clinical training

### Lab safety

What the mentor provides before lab work starts, such as lab-specific training, hazard information, required institutional training and protective equipment. What the student is responsible for. How to report safety concerns.

### Funding

Who pays the stipend, health insurance and tuition. When that changes. Any funding the mentor must show before a trainee joins the lab. Fellowship supplements, if any.

### Required program activities

Seminars, retreats, courses, ethics and rigor training and any clinical refresher before returning to clinical training.

### Time away

The vacation allowance, what doesn't count toward it, how to give notice, how to handle unexpected absences and that more leave can be agreed between the student and mentor. Parental, medical and family leave should point to the institution's official policies.

### Clinical and career activities

For dual-degree programs: how students keep up clinical interests, such as shadowing or teaching. How these are agreed with the mentor.

### If concerns come up

Who to contact if expectations become misaligned. Raising concerns early is encouraged.

### Contacts

Names, roles and email addresses for program leadership. Show email addresses as plain text.

### Signatures

Student and mentor, plus a second mentor if co-mentored. The date the compact is due.

### Questions for both people

Each person answers on their own. The questions are the same for both, written so either can answer. Short answers are fine.

Leave out any question the program layer already settles, like the vacation allowance or milestone dates. Keep questions about how the pair will work within those rules.

### Meeting and communication

- How often should we meet one on one? For how long?
- How quickly do you expect a reply to email or messages? Are evenings and weekends ever expected?
- How should the trainee share progress between meetings?
- How would you like to give and receive feedback?

### Research and the project

- Who chooses the project and the questions within it?
- How much should the trainee work independently? When should they ask for help?
- Where are data, notebooks and code kept? Who can see them?
- What happens to the project if the trainee leaves?

### Authorship and sharing work

- How will authorship and author order be decided?
- When can the trainee present the work at meetings or post a preprint?
- Can the trainee take the project, or part of it, when they leave?

### Time and working hours

- What are typical working hours? How flexible are they?
- How far ahead should vacation be planned?
- How should the trainee tell the mentor about time away?

### Training and career

- What are the trainee's career goals right now?
- Which conferences, courses or workshops should the trainee attend? Who pays?
- When and how will the trainee apply for fellowships, such as an F30, F31 or F32?
- How will the trainee's individual development plan be used and reviewed?

### For MD-PhD students

- How will the trainee keep up clinical skills during the research years?
- What is the target date for finishing the PhD and returning to clinical training? What has to happen first?

### The lab

- What does the trainee need to know about how the lab runs, such as shared tasks, ordering and safety?
- How does the lab make sure everyone feels welcome and able to speak up?

### When things get hard

- If one of us is unhappy with how things are going, how should we raise it?
- Who else can the trainee go to for advice, such as committee members or the program director?

### Comparing the answers

Put both people's answers side by side, one topic per row.

| Topic | Mentor | Trainee | Group |
|---|---|---|---|

### The three groups

- We agree. The answers say the same thing in different words. Write it into the compact as is.
- Small difference. The answers are close, like meeting every week versus every two weeks. Suggest a middle ground for the pair to accept or change.
- Needs a conversation. The answers point in different directions, like who decides author order or whether weekend work is expected. Don't pick a side. Write one or two neutral questions for the pair to discuss.

### Writing neutral questions

- Describe both answers fairly, without saying which is right.
- Ask about the reason behind each answer, not just the answer.
- Look for a solution that meets both needs.
- Example:

One of you wrote that the mentor decides author order at the end of a project. The other wrote that it should be agreed at the start. What matters most to each of you here? Could you agree on a first plan now and revisit it when the paper is drafted?

### What to look out for

- Topics one person answered and the other left blank. These often matter most.
- Answers that go against institutional policy. Point these out gently and suggest checking the policy.
- Signs that the trainee may not feel able to disagree, such as very short answers that copy the mentor's. If the program director set this up, suggest they check in with the trainee separately.

### Pair layer template

This is the part each mentor and trainee write for themselves. It goes after the program layer, in the space for notes or amendments. Keep it to about one page. Leave out any topic the pair didn't need to settle.

Our agreements [Mentor's name] and [trainee's name], [date]. These add to the program's compact and follow its policies. We will review them by [date].

Meeting and communication We will meet one on one [how often] for [how long]. [Reply times, evenings and weekends.] [How progress is shared between meetings.] [How feedback is given.]

Research [Who chooses the project and questions.] [How independent the trainee will be and when to ask for help.] [Where data and code are kept.]

Authorship and sharing work [How authorship and order are decided.] [Conferences and preprints.] [What happens to the project if the trainee leaves.]

Working hours and time away [Typical hours and flexibility.] [How vacation is planned, within the program's allowance.]

Training and career [The trainee's current goals.] [Conferences, courses and who pays for them.] [Fellowship plans and timing.] [How the development plan is reviewed.]

Clinical activities (for dual-degree trainees) [Which clinical or teaching activities and how often.]

Lab culture [Shared tasks, lab meetings and anything from the lab's code of conduct.]

### Anything else we agreed

Signed: ____________ (mentor) ____________ (trainee)
